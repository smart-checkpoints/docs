---
title: "How enforcement works"
description: "From a plate at a camera to a recorded violation."
---

Nothing in the chain is clever. Each step does one thing, and the number that
matters, the road distance, is the one the system refuses to guess.

<Steps>
  <Step title="The same vehicle is seen twice">
    Every checkpoint records a plate and the moment it passed, and posts both to
    `/report-checkpoint`. Enforcement begins when one plate appears at two
    checkpoints that share an edge, not when it passes any single camera.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The road distance is resolved">
    The server knows the two checkpoints are connected. It does not know how far
    apart they are by road, so it asks a [distance
    driver](/concepts/distance-drivers), which routes between them and answers
    in metres.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Average speed is computed">
    Distance divided by elapsed time gives the average speed across the whole
    edge. Held against the edge's speed limit, the run either fits inside the
    time the limit allows or it does not.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The run is recorded">
    Every traversal is stored against its edge with its elapsed time. Violations
    are the runs that crossed the edge faster than the limit permits, and they
    are written to the `violations` table and pushed to any connected browser.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## What the server tracks per vehicle

The server keeps exactly one row per plate per project: the last checkpoint it
was seen at, and when. A sighting is therefore always judged against the
previous one, and the plate's history is not replayed.

Three cases produce no violation:

- **No previous sighting.** The plate is new, so there is nothing to measure
  against. The sighting is recorded and the response has `status` of `false`.
- **No edge between the two checkpoints.** The vehicle went somewhere that is
  not enforced. Nothing is measured.
- **A non positive elapsed time.** See below.

## Out of order reports

Checkpoint reports arrive over HTTP, so a later sighting can reach the server
before an earlier one. When the elapsed time since the stored sighting is zero
or negative, the request arrived after a newer one was already processed.

The server ignores that request entirely: no violation, and the stored sighting
is left alone rather than being overwritten with an older timestamp. The
response carries `outOfOrder: true`.

## Congestion

Alongside violations, the server records every traversal with its elapsed time,
and every three seconds it broadcasts a congestion figure per edge over
Socket.IO.

```
congestion = average elapsed time over the last 5 minutes / minimum legal traversal time
```

A value near `1` means traffic is moving at roughly the limit. Higher means
slower than the limit. Traversals older than five minutes are pruned, so the
figure only reflects current conditions.

<Note>
  Congestion is derived from the same traversals that enforcement uses. It is a
  read on the road, not a second measurement system.
</Note>
